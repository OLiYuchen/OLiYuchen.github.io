EcoScan helps shoppers understand the impact behind food choices.

Concept focus

A rapid product concept for transparent food data, scan-based recommendations, and more sustainable purchasing decisions.

Theme
Customer transparency for food insecurity and eco-sustainability
UX Concept Mobile App Rapid Prototype
Team
Team Blue 32: Olivia Feng, Eva Fang, Sophia Tang, and Mengqi Shi.

Role

UX Designer

Helped translate the sustainability problem into app flow, content hierarchy, and prototype screens.

Format

Rapid concept

A hackathon-style project shared as a concise, interview-friendly case study.

Audience

Conscious shoppers

People who care about food security and the environmental impact of their purchases.

Skills

  • Problem framing
  • Mobile information architecture
  • Scan and recommendation flow
  • Sustainability communication

Overview

01

We designed around a common behavior: people want to make better choices, but labels do not explain enough.

EcoScan responds to the gap between sustainable intent and shopping reality. Food packaging may show ingredients, claims, or price, but shoppers rarely get a clear view of water pollution, feed use, local sourcing, animal welfare, or other factors that shape the real impact of a purchase.

Problem statement

Large-scale factory farming creates environmental consequences, while shoppers lack accessible, transparent information at the moment of choice.

Purpose

Provide transparency in food choice so consumers can quickly identify products that meet their standards for quality, sustainability, and ethical production.

Product idea

Scan a product or QR code, set personal sustainability concerns, and receive recommendations with simple explanations rather than opaque scores.

Experience Flow

02

The flow moves from personal priorities to scan-based guidance.

The concept avoids a one-size-fits-all definition of sustainability. Instead, the onboarding lets shoppers choose what they care about, then the scan experience translates product data into recommendations that match those priorities.

Step 1

Set priorities

Users select concerns such as health, water pollution, packaging, biodiversity, animal welfare, and chemical use.

Step 2

Scan the product

The app recognizes a label or QR code and turns product information into a readable sustainability summary.

Step 3

Compare and learn

Recommendations, farmer information, and micro-tips help shoppers understand why a choice may be better.

Input

Personal sustainability profile

The preference setup creates a reason for recommendations to be personal, not generic.

Action

Camera or QR scan

The scan interaction reduces effort at the point where shoppers usually have the least time.

Output

Transparent rationale

Results are paired with short explanations so the app teaches future decision-making, not just one-off classification.

Design Decisions

03

I focused the design on making environmental information feel actionable.

The challenge was not only showing more data. It was deciding which data a shopper could understand quickly, how to connect it to personal values, and how to avoid moralizing the experience.

Decision 01

Start with user concerns

Preference bubbles make sustainability multidimensional: users can care about packaging, feed equality, nutrition, animal welfare, or contamination.

Decision 02

Use scanning as a bridge

The camera interaction connects the physical grocery environment to digital explanation without asking users to search manually.

Decision 03

Pair score with context

Profile and product screens include why a product ranks well, plus farmer and local food system context.

Decision 04

Keep the tone encouraging

The interface frames sustainable action as easier and more transparent, not as a guilt-heavy checklist.

Outcome

04

The final concept communicates a product direction, not a fully validated system.

Because EcoScan was a rapid concept, I am presenting it as a concise case study rather than overstating validation. The strongest design value is the product framing: personalized transparency, scan-based decision support, and sustainability education at the point of purchase.

What worked

The concept makes complex food-system information more approachable through personal concerns, visual categorization, and short recommendation logic.

Next step

I would test whether shoppers trust the recommendation rationale, understand the sustainability categories, and feel helped rather than judged.
