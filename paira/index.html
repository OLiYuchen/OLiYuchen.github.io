Overview

01

I joined before there was a product, and helped decide what Paira should be.

Paira started with one ambition: make it easy for a student or new graduate to reach the one person who can actually help them, without the performance that networking usually demands. When I joined in August 2025 there was an idea and a few screens, so I started close to zero. The direction changed several times on the way: from a swipe deck borrowed from dating apps, to seeing that swiping was not friendly to mentors, to understanding what people actually needed, to request-based matching, an AI plan, and a warm introduction. Each step was designed, reviewed, tested with users, and improved. In a startup this small, one of two designers does many jobs. Mine were to make the idea concrete, to own the surfaces where people enter and give help, to draw the brand, and later to run the launch and marketing creative. A year on, Paira is live on the App Store, 100+ people joined the waitlist and 50+ tested the beta, and the product is still moving, so my work on it is too.

People do not need more connections. They need the right person for one real need, at the moment they have it.

Market Research

02

The research said the need was real, and that existing tools were built for the wrong moment.

Before the product had a shape, we had to make the case for it. The early research I contributed to named three problems. Cold outreach on the big professional networks feels like shouting into the void. Formal mentors are expensive, scarce, or locked behind selective programs. The objection we had to answer

“I think this is a good idea, but I’m not sure how different it is from [the big networks]. They already allow cold messaging through paid features. The challenge might be making connections feel more reachable and approachable.”

A participant in the first round of interviews

So we positioned Paira differently

Relational, not transactional. One need at a time, not a whole career.

The big professional and campus networks are broad but transactional: you browse a marketplace of people and send requests into it. Leland and ADPList sell a booked session. Lunchclub and PolyWorks are relational but built around one format each. Paira takes the empty quadrant: relational and multi-purpose, peers helping peers on a specific need, with the introduction handled for you. That is what makes a connection feel reachable, which is exactly what the interviews asked for.

“Find the help. Be the help.”

The line the beta guide launched with in February 2026. Getting career help should feel natural and mutual: ask when you need it, offer when you are ready, without pressure or formality.

The Pivot

03

We changed the unit of the product from a person to a request, and I redesigned my surfaces around it.

The early Paira borrowed its shape from dating apps: a People tab you swiped through, a Request tab with small bounty posts, and AI promised for later. We shipped that version to beta testers over TestFlight in early 2026. People liked the demo, but they did not come back. Even after two people matched, they still had to think of a reason to talk. We moved to a request-based product step by step, through several rounds of design, review, and user testing. The round of user tests below is the one that made the decision clear.

What a round of user tests told us

  • Swiping had friction. Each card demanded a decision right away, and that pressure made people quit instead of choose.
  • Everyone wanted to know more. In a job search, a headline is not enough to say yes or no to a person.
  • The mentor side was inefficient. Helpers had to swipe through people with nothing concrete to respond to, so the deck went stale on their end.
  • A match was not a conversation. Two people who liked each other still had to invent the first message.

We designed and ran the tests as a design team: one-on-one sessions with potential users, a think-aloud protocol, and set tasks to complete. We brought the findings to the team and shaped the new direction together.

In spring 2026 we rebuilt the product around the request.

What changed

What that means in the product.

AI does the writing
It asks two or three clarifying questions and drafts the request. When a helper is chosen, it writes a warm introduction and a short action plan, so the first message is already there.

We made that call together. My part was to carry it into the surfaces I own, and the next section walks through each one.

How a request is born now

One loose sentence, two quick questions, a request worth answering.

A replay of the seeker flow the team shipped. Pick an answer to steer it, or let it run.

  1. Say it the way you would to a friend.No form, no fields. The sentence is the only required input.
  2. Two clarifying questions, with answers you can tap.Stage and scope are the two things the matching needs; everything else the AI infers.
  3. The draft appears as editable fields.Title, description, type, industry, location. The AI writes, the person still owns it.
  4. Post, and see who can help.Helpers arrive with a reason each. The first message is already written.

What happens after you post

A request stays legible from posting to conversation.

Paira’s core loop, shared across the team. A post lands in a helper’s Explore, a reply carries a reason, one helper is chosen and the others are told, and Paira AI writes the introduction. Every surface I own sits somewhere on this line.

The pivot made intent visible. It lowered the social cost of asking, and it gave the AI something better than a profile to work with.

Core Experience

04

I own the surfaces where people enter Paira, find something to help with, and decide to give more.

Five surfaces, in the order a person meets them: onboarding, Explore, profile, premium, and the mentor-side AI flow. Each one had to be rethought when the product moved to requests, and each one shows a different part of that shift.

3.1

Onboarding

Ask what someone wants to do before asking for a résumé.

Sign-up is short on purpose: phone, a six-digit code, an email, a name. Then the tone changes. A “Heyyy :)” splash, a photo and a one-line headline, and the screen that matters: So, what’s on your radar? You pick what you can do (mentor, mentee, peer, co-founder) and what you want to do (mock interview, networking, coffee chat, resume review). Industries, notifications, and a referral code worth 50 free points close the loop.

Why. Intentions are the only profile data the matching needs on day one. Everything else can wait until it will actually improve a match. The goal is that a new user reaches a drafted request before they have written a bio.

What changed at the pivot. The same intention chips used to feed a swipe deck. Now they seed the AI’s first question and the helper’s tag list.

3.2

Explore, “Your Daily Picks”

A short, daily, refreshable list of requests I could help with, instead of an infinite feed of people.

Explore is the helper’s front door. Each day it shows a handful of open requests matched to you, and each one carries a badge that says why it is there. You can save a pick before it refreshes away, pull to refresh, and when the day’s picks run out the screen tells you when the next batch arrives. Tapping a pick opens the request. “I’m Interested” moves it into your Request Hub as something you are helping with.

The limits are the point. A small daily set makes helping feel like a favor you can finish, not a queue you owe. Badges replace a match percentage with a reason, so the AI’s judgment stays readable. The first visit shows a two-step tutorial, because saving and refreshing are the only gestures the surface asks you to learn.

What changed at the pivot. The old Request tab was a swipe deck of bounties. Explore is a list you read, save, and return to, and every card is something you can act on today.

3.3

Profile

A profile that reads as “what I can help with,” not a résumé to be judged.

The profile leads with two lists, “Can help with” and “Want to do,” an “Open to networking” switch, and recommendations written by people you have actually worked with. Experience and education are there, but lower down, and the editor breaks them into small steps so nobody stalls on a blank form. Points, Request Boost, and settings also live here, because this is where a person decides how much of themselves to put into the platform. The profile is never demanded up front. Explore asks you to complete it at the moment it starts to matter, when mentees are deciding whether to trust you.

Why. After the pivot, a profile is read mostly by someone deciding whether to accept your help, or by the AI deciding whether to suggest you. Both need to know what you can do, not where you went to school.

3.4

Premium: points, Boost, and paid pings Designed, awaiting release

Make the paid layer about your request getting seen, not about unlocking other people.

Paira’s premium layer runs on points. You earn them by inviting friends who complete a profile. You spend them to boost a request for 24 hours, to ping someone without a match, or to see who has “Paira’d” you. Boost is the piece I care most about after the pivot. It is the one paid action that helps the request rather than the ego, so it keeps the product’s promise. The purple sheet and the “Request Boosted!” moment are meant to feel like a small celebration, not a checkout.

Open question. Premium Ping and “See who Paira’d you” come from the match era. I am watching whether they still make sense in a request-first product, or whether the paid layer should narrow to Boost and request visibility.

3.5

Mentor-side AI flow

Let a helper describe what they are good at in one paragraph, and have AI turn it into tags and matches.

The seeker’s AI flow gets most of the attention, because it turns “I need help” into a request. The mentor side is its mirror, and it is mine. We wanted it fast. It opens on suggestion bubbles built from your onboarding intentions and past preferences, sized by how likely each one is to fit, so most helpers reach their matches in two taps. “Tell Us More” is the slower path: a paragraph in your own words, which the AI reads and turns into tags you can edit before it shows the mentees waiting for you.

The design problem was symmetry without sameness. A helper has less patience for a questionnaire than a seeker does, so the AI shows its work for a beat and then gets out of the way. The outcome is a list of concrete things to do, not people to judge. At the end the flow asks one question, whether to save these answers to your profile, and that is how the mentor flow feeds Explore.

Shown here as core functions, designed with the team rather than owned by me: the request status lifecycle, the seeker-side AI request flow, Request Hub, chat with Paira AI action plans, meeting scheduling, and the helper profile’s “Why you are seeing this person” card.

Brand & Motion

06

I designed Paira to look like the opposite of a corporate networking room, then kept it consistent across the app, the site, and the street.

Paira lives on near-black with two voices: an acid yellow for the person asking, and a soft violet for the person helping and for the AI. Headlines are Playfair Display in italic and body text is Plus Jakarta Sans, which lets a screen feel editorial without feeling formal. The mark is two rounded shapes that overlap, one blue, one yellow: the person asking and the person helping. I drew the logo and the icon family, then built the loading animation so the same two shapes do the waiting. The component library is shared with the team. The identity is mine.

Latest update · Sep 2026

Exploring AI as a production partner: a two-screen demo film, directed by me, built by an agent.

When Apple announced the iPhone Duo, I storyboarded a story with the mentee on the left screen and the mentor on the right, and had Claude Code build it from our real product assets. My job was the brief, the assets, and the taste. The agent’s job was execution.

  1. Brief and storyboard. Seven scenes with timings, the moments that cross the fold, and a clear list of what the agent can and cannot do (no 3D, no illustration, no voice).
  2. Asset library. Figma frames exported. The app’s real screens recorded from the React prototype with a shot-list rig, where a JSON list of taps drives the UI and frames come out at 3x. Brand Lottie files, my character illustrations, and Apple’s device bezel.
  3. Agent build. Claude Code composes an HTML timeline: two panels, a request card that is generated on one screen and flies across the fold, and chat that alternates between the two.
  4. Render. Headless Chrome renders the timeline frame by frame, and ffmpeg assembles the mp4 with music.
  5. Direct, don’t edit. I review each cut and give notes on smoothness, occlusion, sharpness, and pace, and the agent revises. Iterations are cheap, so the notes get sharper each round.

Reflection

09

A pivot is only real when the smallest surfaces agree with it.

Paira taught me to hold two altitudes at once: the product-level call to build around a request, and the screen-level work of making that call felt in an onboarding step, a badge label, or a points menu. Direction and interface are the same decision made at two scales. It also taught me what a pivot leaves behind. Premium Ping and “See who Paira’d you” still speak the language of matching. The mentor-side AI flow has shipped, and I want to see it earn its place with real helpers. Those are the next things I want to fix. The product is still moving, so I can.

What I would keep

The request as the unit of the product, badges that explain instead of score, and a daily Explore that ends. Constraints made the helper side feel finishable.

What I would change next

Narrow the paid layer to request visibility, retire the match-era features, and measure how much profile a first request really needs.
