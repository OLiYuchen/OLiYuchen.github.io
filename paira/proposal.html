What the beta taught us

Signal 1

Browsing people stalled.

  • Swiping had friction. Each card demanded a decision right away, and that pressure made people quit instead of choose.
  • Everyone wanted to know more. In a job search, a headline is not enough to say yes or no to a person.

Signal 2

Real requests got answered.

  • A specific need started real conversations. Of the beta’s three features, requests drew the strongest response, on both sides.
  • The mentor side needed the same clarity. Helpers swiping through people had nothing concrete to respond to; helpers reading a request did.

Signal 3

A match was not yet a conversation.

  • Two people who liked each other still had to invent the first message.
  • One-shot AI was not enough. Search understood the intent, then left people on their own. Intent needed a guided path from the ask to the match.

We designed and ran the tests as a design team: one-on-one sessions with potential users, a think-aloud protocol, and set tasks to complete, on top of 30+ user interviews across both chapters. We brought the findings to the team and shaped the new direction together.

4.3

Profile

A profile that reads as “what I can help with,” filled in when it starts to matter.

The profile leads with two lists, “Can help with” and “Want to do,” an “Open to networking” switch, and recommendations written by people you have actually worked with. Experience and education are there, but lower down, and the editor breaks them into small steps so nobody stalls on a blank form. Points, Request Boost, and settings also live here, because this is where a person decides how much of themselves to put into the platform. The profile is never demanded up front; the app asks you to complete it at the moment it starts to matter, when mentees are deciding whether to trust you.

Why. A profile is read by someone deciding whether to accept your help, or by the AI deciding whether to suggest you. Both need to know what you can do, not where you went to school. Asking for it later, in context, is what lets onboarding stay short without losing match quality.

Trust signals. Under the headline sit the numbers a mentee reads before saying yes: requests helped, likes received, days on Paira, and average response time. Recommendations from people you actually helped come next, and the “Open to” switches say what you offer, so nobody asks for what you did not. Trust is built into the profile rather than promised in a bio.

How I grew

  1. From collaborator to shipper. I worked with the PM and the developers on requirements from the first frame to the App Store, and often was the PM, and half a developer, when the work needed it.
  2. Finding better ways to work together. We moved from everyone working separately and posting updates, to using AI to carry ideas, and to putting the prototype on the web so the whole team could brainstorm and vibe-code on the same screens.
  3. Defining the product, and my place in it. In mixed roles and an unknown market, I learned to help decide what Paira should become next, and where my strengths are most useful: product judgment, systems, and the craft of the screen.

What I learned at work

  1. A cleaner path from design to code. As the app grew, the hard questions became: where is the latest screen, what is in development, what is built but still has issues. I organized the current screens and wrote a simple set of rules each role follows, so everyone stays in sync.
    Meeting→AI notes and decision log→Ticket→Prototype on the web→Latest-screen inventory→Role rules for sync→iOS build
  2. One design system, app and web. Bringing the app’s system to the web version made the marketing site faster to build, and made this case study possible.
  3. AI as a working partner. I keep testing where AI helps UI/UX design and where it does not, and I build my own small tools and agents along the way, so my learning compounds with the team’s.
