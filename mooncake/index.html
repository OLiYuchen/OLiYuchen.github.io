Orchid Mooncake

A mid-autumn mooncake mould for Halekulani, Waikiki, traced from the hotel's Cattleya orchid and engineered to survive vacuum forming.

One printed buck pulls a plastic negative, and the negative casts the plaster cake. Everything the cake shows has to be designed into the first part.

Sole designer and fabricator. Concept, CAD, print, forming, casting.
Process
CAD model → FDM printed buck → vacuum formed sheet → plaster cast.
Tools

01

Reading a logo as a mould

Halekulani means house befitting heaven. It is a quiet hotel on the beach at Waikiki, and its whole identity rests on one flower: the white Cattleya orchid in the logo, the restaurant named after it, and over a million glass tiles of it at the bottom of the pool.

The governing constraint

A formed sheet only records height

Vacuum forming pulls a flat sheet down onto a shape. Anything shallower than the sheet can follow simply disappears, and anything that wraps under itself traps the sheet and keeps it there. That turns a graphic identity question into a geometry question. Strokes under 1 mm vanish. A dotted ring is far too fine. A silhouette and one clear step survive.

So the brief reduced to one decision: which parts of the crest are heights, and which parts are only drawing.

Kept as geometry

  • The orchid, as a 2 mm relief.
  • The scalloped border, which also reads as the fluted edge of a mooncake.
  • White, which is the colour plaster already is.

Left as drawing

  • Lettering, with strokes under 1 mm.
  • The dotted ring, too fine for the sheet to reach.
  • Palm trees, which say Hawaii rather than Halekulani.

02

One shape, three materials

The part I model is the part the plaster becomes. In between, a plastic sheet has to come off it and a plaster cake has to come out of that. Each handover sets a rule, and every rule has to be in the model before anything is printed.

  • Model the positive The plaster comes out the same shape as the print, so the buck is designed as the cake, not as the mould.
  • Draft every outer wall A straight wall grips. An angled one lets the sheet lift off and the cake slide out.
  • No undercuts The sheet cannot release anything it has wrapped under, so no geometry may overhang itself.
  • Give air a way out Every recess needs its own path to a vent, or trapped air holds the sheet off the surface.

  1. Print the buck

    A PLA positive with vent holes running from the base through to the pattern recess.

  2. Vacuum form

    A heated sheet is pulled down over the buck and the air is drawn out through the vents.

  3. Pour plaster

    The formed sheet becomes the negative. Flipped over, it is filled with plaster.

  4. Demould

    The same draft that released the sheet releases the cured plaster cake.

03

One file, many versions

Rather than save a new model for every idea, I built one base part and used Fusion configurations to switch features on and off inside it. Each test print is a configuration, so the versions stay comparable and a change to the shared geometry reaches all of them at once.

Blank base, raised relief, engraved, engraved with a finer mesh, and the final raised version. Height, motif depth, rim radius, wall draft and hole count are all read straight out of the model rather than retyped, so the table and the part cannot drift apart.

04

Giving the air a way out

The first round of prints settled the question I had been going back and forth on, and it was not the one I expected to answer. Raised against engraved turned out not to matter. What mattered was whether air could leave the recess.

4.1

Round one

The outline formed. The orchid stayed flat.

Does the motif read better raised or engraved?

I printed three bucks at once: blank, raised orchid and engraved orchid. The outline and the scallops came through on all of them, and the orchid came through on none. The vents were specified at Ø0.4, which is below what the printer can hold open, so they closed up as they printed. With no exit, the air sitting in the 2 mm recesses held the sheet up and it bridged straight across the motif.

The rims also gripped on release. At 22 mm tall with only 2° of draft, the sheet had very little room to let go of the scallops.

What it settled Two independent variables, cleanly separated: hole size controls whether the motif forms at all, and draft angle controls whether the sheet releases. Neither has anything to do with raised against engraved, so that question could be decided on looks alone.

4.2

The pocket nothing could reach

The recess is two regions, not one

Enlarging the holes to Ø1 made them print open, and six of them on a bolt circle covered most of the recessed field. Before committing I checked the floor of the recess for connectivity rather than assuming it was a single region, and it is not. The raised lip of the orchid, the heart shaped petal, closes a contour around itself. Inside that contour sits a 66.5 mm² pocket walled off from the 900.3 mm² main field by the full 2 mm step, with no path between them at any point.

No hole on the ring can serve it. So the vent count went to eight: six on the bolt circle, one inside the lip pocket, and one at the orchid centre.

Why it is worth drawing The two areas sum to 966.8 mm² against a measured recess floor of 966.7, which confirms the split is real and complete rather than an artefact of how the surface was read.

4.3

Draft

From 2° to 4°, and from 22 mm to 20

With the vents opened up, release became the remaining variable. At 2° the buck had to be worked out of the sheet. Taking the wall to 4° and the height down to 20 mm lifts it off cleanly, and at that angle the scallops are still crisp enough that the change is invisible in the finished cake.

The angle is small enough to be worth checking rather than trusting. Across the 18 mm drafted wall the base radius drops by 1.2585 mm, and the arctangent of that over the height returns 4.0000°, so what the drawing claims and what the model holds are the same number.

05

Printing, forming, casting

Six bucks came off the printer across the two rounds. The final version was formed repeatedly and cast in batches, which is where the last few notes came from.

Noted at the bench

Fill line

Some cups went over and plaster spilled onto the flange. A marked fill line on the sheet would make the pour repeatable and keep the rim clean.

Noted at the bench

Draft works twice

The 4° that frees the sheet from the buck also frees the plaster from the sheet, so one decision pays for itself at both handovers.

06

Lip, petals and flutes all came through

The cast cake carries the full orchid, the heart shaped lip that needed its own vent, and all twenty eight flutes around the edge. Held next to the logo it reads as the same flower.

Up close

Layer lines

The sheet copies the print layers on the wall, so the side of the cake carries the buck's finish. Sanding the buck before forming would read straight through to a smooth flute.

Up close

Petal texture

Print texture shows faintly across the orchid. Priming the buck is the next thing to try, since surface quality travels down the whole chain from the first part.

07

The drawing

A drawing that someone else could build from: three orthographic views and an isometric, the draft angle and its pull direction, the vent pattern with its bolt circle, and notes that say which geometry is dimensioned and which is carried by the model.

Mooncake forming buck, Cattleya orchid HLP-C03-001 Rev 1.0 A3, first angle Units mm

Dimensioned

  • Base circle Ø50 and scalloped outer circle Ø51.70, so the controlling circle and the real outer size are both visible.
  • Total height 20, drafted wall 18, pattern recess 2.
  • Vent bolt circle Ø39.13 at 60°, with the two centre holes placed from the axis.
  • Scallop arc R2.92 on a Ø45.86 centre circle, in a detail view.

Deliberately not dimensioned

  • The orchid outline and the edge of the pattern field. Both were traced from artwork rather than constructed, and the field radius wanders by about 0.6 mm around the circle.
  • Putting a diameter on a traced curve would invent a tolerance nobody can hold, so those are called out as decorative profile per the model instead.

08

What I took from it

This was my first part designed around a forming process rather than around how it looks, and most of what I learned sits in the gap between a model that is correct and a model that can actually be made.

How I work now

  1. I design the handovers, not just the part. Every rule in the model traces back to a moment where one material has to let go of another.
  2. I check the thing I am about to assume. The recess looked like one region, and analysing it rather than trusting it is what found the sealed pocket.
  3. I keep versions in one file. Configurations made five test prints comparable, and kept the parameter table honest because it reads from the model.

What I would try next

  1. Prime and sand the buck before forming, since surface finish travels all the way down to the cast cake.
  2. Add a moulded fill line to the sheet so the pour is repeatable and the flange stays clean.
  3. Test the draft angle in smaller steps between 2° and 4°, to find the shallowest wall that still releases.
