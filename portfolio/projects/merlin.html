Executive Summary

Designing for beginners meant looking beyond identification accuracy.

Merlin Bird ID already helps millions identify birds by photo, sound, or a step-by-step flow. The Cornell Lab wanted to reach a specific, under-served group — young, novice birders — and understand what keeps them engaged long enough to keep learning and caring about the natural world.

Design question

How might we design Merlin Bird ID as a companion app to young, novice birdwatchers (18–40) that encourages regular engagement and long-term behavioral change toward more environmentally conscious lifestyles?

Synthesis & design

Project Introduction

A research-led capstone, not a shipped feature.

Merlin Bird ID, a free app from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, has more than three million users and helps birdwatchers of all levels. But it's a companion for identification — the Lab is especially interested in recruiting and retaining young, novice birdwatchers who may not share the background knowledge of seasoned birders.

We met weekly with Dr. Shana Hirsch of the EcoFutures Design Lab and collaborated with the Cornell Lab's UI designers. Our deliverable was a grounded understanding of novice birders plus a set of defensible, tested design directions the Merlin team could carry forward.

Research Methods

We triangulated behavior across surveys, the field, and media.

The survey helped us recruit and compare participants by bird interest, knowledge, and how often they birdwatch. Field observations captured tools, behavior, and context in the wild, and media analysis widened the picture of novice birding culture. We then used grounded-theory methods — transcript coding, thematic analysis, and affinity mapping — to turn raw data into patterns.

Summary of Findings

Confidence depends on context, identity, and support — not just accuracy.

Novices don't simply need better identification output. They need support that matches their knowledge, tools, and social situation — many don't even call themselves “birdwatchers.”

01

Spontaneous & social

Many novices notice birds during daily life rather than planned outings, and enter birding through friends.

02

No single journey

People arrive with different identities and motivations, so Merlin can't assume one path from beginner to expert.

03

Barriers beyond UI

Time, tool access, knowledge gaps, information overload, and weather all shape how confident a novice feels.

04

Community shapes identity

Some novices want encouragement; others feel intimidated by experts and fear getting IDs wrong.

05

Layered knowledge gaps

They may know birds casually but not know what they don't know — advanced ID, local behavior, community.

06

Six types, not one

Bird enjoyers, caretakers, nature lovers, aesthetic enjoyers, curious learners, companions — each with a different goal.

Design Implications

From findings to guardrails for design.

Before jumping to features, I helped translate research into implications — the things any Merlin concept for novices should respect.

For the mobile experience

Not all target users call themselves “birdwatchers.” For many, identification isn't the end goal — it's a step in learning about a bird. Novices are easily overwhelmed by information, sometimes rely on external motivation, and often bird spontaneously without tools like binoculars.

For broader engagement

“Knowledge level” means different things to different people. Many novices enjoy learning in community where they can ask questions, yet fear being wrong in front of experts. Their curiosity often extends beyond birds to plants, animals, and the outdoors.

Design Directions

Four concept directions, each answering a real barrier.

We framed beginner support as an ongoing system across three opportunity areas — motivation & habit, learning basics, and social sharing — and explored four concepts grounded in the research.

Idea 01 · Social

Events & Community

Lightweight events users can find, favorite, and share — answering how much community matters, without heavy social backend.

Idea 02 · Motivation

Badges & Achievements

Rarity badges, weekly challenges, and shareable birding recaps to support motivation and goals beyond identification.

Idea 03 · Learning

Flashcards & Quizzes

ID summaries and self-testing flashcards that break information into smaller pieces — a response to feeling overwhelmed.

Idea 04 · Learning

“Learning Mode”

A toggleable mode with embedded tips and modules — helping with the parts of birding beyond ID, like finding birds or decoding jargon.

Testing & Feedback

Polarizing results confirmed there is no single beginner.

After sponsor feedback, we ran four rounds of testing with our original interviewees, categorized by novice type. Every tester had a different favorite feature — consistent with the kind of birder they are. Rather than crowning one winner, that polarization became the point: it validated a flexible system where novices opt into the support that fits them.

My Reflection

Leading research is less about collecting more data and more about protecting the question.

Leading research means saying no

With a broad prompt and an eager team, my job was often to narrow scope — which questions actually move the design, and which are just interesting. Defining that early kept our synthesis sharp.

Ambiguity is the work, not a detour

Our polarizing test results could have felt like a failure. Reading them as a finding — that novices are plural, not one persona — changed how I frame “inconclusive” results now.

Stakeholders need a story, not a data dump

Presenting weekly to Dr. Hirsch and translating for Cornell's designers taught me to connect sponsor goals, scope, and evidence into one narrative the whole room could follow.

Rotating the PM role made me a better teammate

Leading one milestone and being led in others gave me real empathy for planning under deadline — shaping how I hand off work and keep a team unblocked ever since.
