Merlin Bird ID: helping young novice birdwatchers feel less overwhelmed and more engaged in the field.

For my HCDE senior capstone, I led UX research and shared product management in a team of four, partnering with the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and the UW EcoFutures Design Lab. We spent six months studying what it actually means to be a young, novice birder, then translated that research into product directions Merlin could use to build confidence, learning, and long-term engagement.

My role

UX Research Lead & rotating Product Manager (team of 4)

Partners

Cornell Lab of Ornithology & UW EcoFutures Design Lab

Timeline

Sep 2024–Jun 2025 · 6-month capstone

Methods

Surveys, field observation, interviews, synthesis, concept design, user testing

Overview

Designing for beginners meant looking beyond identification accuracy.

Merlin Bird ID is a free bird-identification app from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology with more than three million users. It already helps people identify birds by photo, sound, or a step-by-step flow. But the Lab wanted to understand a specific, under-served group: young, novice birdwatchers aged 18–40 — what motivates them, where they get stuck, and what keeps them engaged long enough to keep learning and caring about the natural world.

Design question

How might we design Merlin Bird ID as a companion app to young, novice birdwatchers (18–40) that encourages regular engagement and long-term behavioral changes toward more environmentally conscious lifestyles?

This was intentionally a research-led capstone. Our deliverable wasn’t a shipped feature — it was a grounded understanding of novice birders plus a set of defensible, tested design directions the Merlin team could carry forward.

My Role

My main responsibility: own the research direction and keep the product story honest.

Our capstone rotated the formal project-manager role across four milestones, so each of us led the team at least once. Beyond that shared rhythm, my consistent responsibility was to steer the research — deciding what we needed to learn, protecting scope, and making sure our design directions stayed anchored to real evidence rather than assumptions.

Led the research direction

I framed the learning agenda — motivations, barriers, identity, and real-world field behavior — so we studied what it means to be a novice birder, not just how usable the app is. I helped design the survey, field-observation protocol, and interview guide.

Drove synthesis and product framing

I guided grounded-theory synthesis — transcript coding, thematic analysis, and affinity mapping — and connected sponsor goals, team scope, and evidence into a single product story the team could defend to Cornell and the EcoFutures Lab.

Managed milestones and stakeholders

During my PM milestone I ran planning and delivery, and throughout the project I prepared and led our weekly check-ins with Dr. Shana Hirsch and coordinated feedback from Cornell’s UI designers so the team stayed unblocked and aligned.

Translated findings into design action

I turned the research into design implications for onboarding, confidence-building, learning support, and engagement, then helped shape the four concept directions we tested with users.

Process

A mixed-method study, structured in three phases.

We spent the first stretch of the capstone forming the team, securing sponsors, and scoping the problem, then ran two main phases — research and design — with a capstone showcase at the end. The research phase moved from broad discovery to deep, contextual interviews before converging in synthesis.

01

Discovery

Surveys, field observations, and media & literature review to build a broad picture of who novice birders are and how they behave.

02

Interviews

Interviews and shadowing sessions with six novice birders (18–40) to understand their histories, habits, tools, and pain points in context.

03

Synthesis & design

Grounded-theory synthesis into key findings, then concept directions tested across four rounds of user feedback.

Research Methods

We triangulated behavior across surveys, the field, and media.

The survey helped us recruit and compare participants along measures like bird interest, knowledge, and how often and how long they birdwatch. Field observations let us study tools, behavior, and context in the wild, while media analysis expanded the picture of novice birding culture and entry points beyond direct sessions. We then used grounded-theory methods — transcript coding, thematic analysis, and affinity mapping — to turn raw data into patterns.

What We Learned

Confidence depends on context, identity, and support — not just accuracy.

The clearest outcome was that novice birdwatchers don’t simply need better identification output. They need support that matches their knowledge, tools, and social situation. Many participants didn’t even identify with the term “birdwatcher,” which matters for how a product frames itself and invites people in.

01

Birding is spontaneous and social

Many novices notice birds during daily life rather than planned outings, and often enter birding through friends or shared moments.

02

There’s no single novice journey

People arrive with different identities, motivations, and commitment, so Merlin can’t assume one path from beginner to expert.

03

Barriers go beyond app UI

Time, tool access, knowledge gaps, information overload, weather, and social dynamics all shape how confident a novice feels.

04

Community shapes identity and learning

Some novices want encouragement and conversation; others feel intimidated by experts and fear getting IDs wrong.

05

Novices have layered knowledge gaps

They may know birds casually but not know what they don’t know — advanced ID features, local behavior, or where to find community.

06

Six types, not one beginner

Bird enjoyers, caretakers, nature lovers, aesthetic enjoyers, curious learners, and companions — each with a different goal for watching birds.

Design Implications

From findings to guardrails for design.

Before jumping to features, I helped translate the research into implications — the things any Merlin concept for novices should respect. They fell into two buckets: how the mobile experience should behave, and how Merlin might support engagement beyond identification.

For the mobile experience

Not all target users call themselves “birdwatchers.” For many, identification isn’t the end goal — it’s a step in learning more about a bird. Novices are easily overwhelmed by information, sometimes rely on external motivation, and often bird spontaneously without specialized tools like binoculars.

For broader engagement

“Knowledge level” means different things to different people. Many novices enjoy learning in community where they can ask questions, yet fear being wrong in front of experts. Their curiosity often extends beyond birds to plants, animals, and the outdoors more broadly.

Design Directions

Four concept directions, each answering a real barrier.

Instead of a single onboarding screen, we framed beginner support as an ongoing system of confidence-building touchpoints across three opportunity areas: motivation and habit building, learning birding basics, and social sharing. We explored four concept directions grounded in the research, designed to be realistic for the Merlin team to consider.

Idea 01 · Social

Events & Community

Lightweight, “pseudo-social” events users can find, favorite, and share — answering how important community is without heavy backend social infrastructure.

Idea 02 · Motivation

Badges & Achievements

Rarity badges, weekly challenges, and shareable birding recaps to support motivation and the many goals people have beyond identification.

Idea 03 · Learning

Flashcards & Quizzes

ID summaries and self-testing flashcards that break information into smaller pieces — a direct response to novices feeling overwhelmed.

Idea 04 · Learning

“Learning Mode”

A toggleable mode with embedded tips and modules — helping with the parts of birding beyond ID, like finding birds or decoding jargon.

Testing & Iteration

Polarizing results confirmed there is no single beginner.

After sponsor feedback, we ran four rounds of user testing with our original interviewees, categorized by novice type. The results were revealing: every tester had a different favorite feature, consistent with the kind of birder they were. Photographers gravitated to ID summaries; companion and curious types leaned into badges and profile customization; event interest was low unless it was social and low-commitment.

Rather than crowning one winner, that polarization became the point — it validated designing a flexible system where novices can opt into the support that fits them, instead of a one-size-fits-all beginner flow.

Outcome

A research-led product direction, handed off to the Merlin team.

We delivered two things the Cornell Lab could act on: a grounded definition of who young novice birdwatchers are and what shapes their experience, plus a set of evidence-backed, user-tested design solutions. Together they gave the Merlin team a clear, defensible starting point rather than another list of assumptions.

My Reflection

This capstone taught me that leading research is less about collecting more data and more about protecting the question — and holding the team’s attention on the people we’re designing for.

Leading research means saying no

The hardest part wasn’t running methods — it was keeping us focused. With a broad prompt and an eager team, my job was often to narrow scope: which questions actually move the design, and which are just interesting. Defining that early kept our synthesis sharp.

Ambiguity is the work, not a detour

Our polarizing test results could have felt like a failure. Learning to read them as a finding — evidence that novices are plural, not a single persona — changed how I frame “inconclusive” results now. Contradiction is often the insight.

Stakeholders need a story, not a data dump

Presenting weekly to Dr. Hirsch and translating for Cornell’s designers taught me to connect sponsor goals, scope, and evidence into one narrative. Product management, I learned, is mostly the discipline of making a team’s decisions legible to the people they affect.

Rotating the PM role made me a better teammate

Leading one milestone and being led in others gave me real empathy for the tradeoffs of planning under deadline. It’s shaped how I hand off work, ask for help, and keep a team unblocked — skills I carried straight into my product work afterward.
