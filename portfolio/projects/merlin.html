Capstone UX Research Lead Product Management Nature Education

Helping young novice birdwatchers feel less overwhelmed in the field.

For my HCDE senior capstone, I led UX research and shared product management in a team of four, partnering with the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and the UW EcoFutures Design Lab. Over six months we studied what it means to be a young, novice birder — then turned that into product directions Merlin could use to build confidence, learning, and lasting engagement.

UX Research Lead & rotating Product Manager (team of 4)

Surveys, field study, interviews, synthesis, concept design, testing

Overview

Designing for beginners meant looking beyond identification accuracy.

Merlin Bird ID already helps millions identify birds by photo, sound, or a step-by-step flow. The Cornell Lab wanted to reach a specific, under-served group — young, novice birdwatchers aged 18–40 — and understand what motivates them, where they get stuck, and what keeps them engaged long enough to keep learning and caring about the natural world.

Design question

How might we design Merlin Bird ID as a companion app to young, novice birdwatchers (18–40) that encourages regular engagement and long-term behavioral change toward more environmentally conscious lifestyles?

This was intentionally a research-led capstone. The deliverable wasn't a shipped feature — it was a grounded understanding of novice birders plus a set of defensible, tested design directions the Merlin team could carry forward.

My Role

My main responsibility: own the research direction and keep the product story honest.

We rotated the formal project-manager role across four milestones, so each of us led the team at least once. Beyond that, my consistent responsibility was steering the research — deciding what we needed to learn, protecting scope, and keeping our design directions anchored to real evidence.

Led the research direction

Framed the learning agenda — motivations, barriers, identity, and field behavior — and helped design the survey, field-observation protocol, and interview guide.

Drove synthesis & product framing

Guided grounded-theory synthesis and connected sponsor goals, scope, and evidence into one product story the team could defend to Cornell and the EcoFutures Lab.

Research

A mixed-method study, grounded in how novices actually behave.

We worked in three phases. Discovery used surveys, field observations, and media review to build a broad picture of the audience. Interviews and shadowing with six novice birders (18–40) added depth on histories, habits, tools, and pain points. Synthesis turned it all into patterns.

In synthesis I guided grounded-theory methods — transcript coding, thematic analysis, and affinity mapping — so raw sessions became findings the whole team could build on.

Findings

Confidence depends on context, identity, and support — not just accuracy.

Novices don't simply need better identification output. They need support that matches their knowledge, tools, and social situation — and many don't even call themselves “birdwatchers.” The clearest pattern: there's no single beginner. We mapped six novice types by their main goal.

01

Spontaneous & social

Many novices notice birds during daily life rather than planned outings, and enter birding through friends.

02

No single journey

People arrive with different identities and motivations, so Merlin can't assume one path from beginner to expert.

03

Barriers beyond UI

Time, tools, knowledge gaps, information overload, and weather all shape how confident a novice feels.

04

Community shapes identity

Some want encouragement; others feel intimidated by experts and fear getting IDs wrong.

05

Layered knowledge gaps

They may know birds casually but not know what they don't know — advanced ID, local behavior, community.

06

Learning over results

For many, identification isn't the end goal — it's one step in learning more about a bird.

Implications

From findings to guardrails for design.

Before jumping to features, I helped translate the research into implications — the things any Merlin concept for novices should respect, in two buckets.

For the mobile experience

Not everyone calls themselves a “birdwatcher.” Novices are easily overwhelmed, sometimes rely on external motivation, and often bird spontaneously without tools like binoculars — so support should reduce overwhelm and meet people mid-moment.

For broader engagement

“Knowledge level” means different things to different people. Many enjoy learning in community where they can ask questions, yet fear being wrong in front of experts. Curiosity often extends beyond birds to nature at large.

Design Directions

Four concept directions, each answering a real barrier.

Instead of a single onboarding screen, we framed beginner support as an ongoing system across three opportunity areas — motivation & habit, learning basics, and social sharing — then designed four concepts grounded in the research.

Idea 01 · Social Events & Community

Lightweight events users can find, favorite, and share — answering how much community matters, without a heavy social backend.

Idea 02 · Motivation Badges & Achievements

Rarity badges, weekly challenges, and shareable birding recaps to support motivation and goals beyond identification.

Idea 03 · Learning Flashcards & Quizzes

ID summaries and self-testing flashcards that break information into smaller pieces — a response to feeling overwhelmed.

Idea 04 · Learning “Learning Mode”

A toggleable mode with embedded tips and modules — helping with the parts of birding beyond ID, like finding birds or decoding jargon.

Testing & Iteration

Polarizing results confirmed there's no single beginner.

After sponsor feedback, we ran four rounds of testing with our original interviewees, grouped by novice type. Every tester had a different favorite feature — consistent with the kind of birder they are. Rather than crowning one winner, that polarization became the point: it validated a flexible system where novices opt into the support that fits them.

Outcome

A research-led product direction, handed off to the Merlin team.

We delivered two things Cornell could act on: a grounded definition of who young novice birdwatchers are and what shapes their experience, plus a set of evidence-backed, user-tested design solutions — a clear, defensible starting point rather than another list of assumptions. We presented it all at the HCDE capstone showcase.

My Reflection

What I took away

Leading research is less about collecting more data and more about protecting the question — and holding the team's attention on the people we're designing for.

Leading research means saying no

With a broad prompt and an eager team, my job was often to narrow scope — which questions actually move the design, and which are just interesting. Defining that early kept our synthesis sharp.

Ambiguity is the work, not a detour

Our polarizing results could have felt like a failure. Reading them as a finding — that novices are plural, not one persona — changed how I frame “inconclusive” results now.

Stakeholders need a story, not a data dump

Presenting weekly to Dr. Hirsch and translating for Cornell's designers taught me to connect sponsor goals, scope, and evidence into one narrative the room could follow.

Rotating the PM role made me a better teammate

Leading one milestone and being led in others gave me real empathy for planning under deadline — shaping how I hand off work and keep a team unblocked ever since.
