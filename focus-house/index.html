Focus House

A small house for the corner of a desk. Sliding its window shut puts the computer into focus mode, so the switch becomes something you do with your hands.

A repurposed mouse PCB sits inside. Closing the window presses its right button, which is mapped to a shortcut that turns on macOS Focus Mode and changes the wallpaper.

01

A gesture instead of a menu

I started with a mouse PCB and a question: what else can a board that only knows how to report a button press be? Putting it inside another mouse shape felt like the least interesting answer, so I looked for an everyday physical action that could stand in for a digital one.

Why a house

Shutting a window is already a thing people understand

A house carries comfort, privacy and the sense of making a space for yourself, and its window gives the object a part that is supposed to move. Closing it reads as shutting out distraction without anyone needing to explain the metaphor.

The form also earns its keep structurally. Building the house from stacked sheet layers leaves a cavity for the PCB and lets the sliding track be cut rather than assembled, so the decorative choice and the mechanical one are the same choice.

The chain

What the gesture actually does

Sliding the window down presses the original right mouse button on the PCB inside. That input is mapped to a keyboard shortcut, which turns on macOS Focus Mode and swaps the desktop wallpaper, so the physical state of the object and the state of the screen agree with each other.

02

From a shape to a stack of layers

Once the house was chosen, the drawing shifted from how it looks to how it is made. Sheet construction means the object has to be described as a set of flat profiles, so I sketched the form from several angles and then cut it apart layer by layer.

These drawings raised the two questions that decided what to prototype first: whether the supporting beams could hold the house square, and what keeps the window in its track.

03

Three tests, three answers

Each prototype was built to settle one question rather than to look like a smaller version of the finished thing. That kept them cheap, and it meant every answer went straight into the model.

3.1

Cardboard

A path is not the same as a constraint

Can the supporting beams hold the structure, and can a window slide without leaving its track?

I cut the whole thing by hand in cardboard before committing to accurate CAD, because at that stage the relationships between the moving parts mattered more than any dimension. Two findings came out of it. The beams needed to be arranged symmetrically and braced in more than one direction to keep the house square. And two bars set into the wall guided the window along the right path while still letting it drop out of the track entirely.

What it changed The slider gained layers on both the inside and the outside, so the window is captured between them. A guide that only defines where a part may travel is doing half the job; it also has to define where the part may not go.

3.2

Laser cut

The board fits, and the kerf is why

Do the PCB dimensions taken from my CAD sketch survive the trip into a real cutout?

I cut the board outline straight from the sketch, and ran an engraving test in the same session to see how engraving could carry detail later. The board dropped into the opening without rework. The clearance it needed came from the process rather than the drawing: the laser removes a little material at the edge and darkens what is left, and that was enough.

What it changed The board outline was frozen and the enclosure was designed around it, instead of the two being adjusted against each other. Accurate CAD gets you close, and the cutting process decides the last fraction, so a fit is worth confirming in the real material before anything depends on it.

3.3

Tolerance

2.90 down to 2.68, in five cuts

What slot width holds the layers together without glue, and still goes together by hand?

Rather than test a generic joint, I cut a miniature version of the actual supporting beam structure, with the same slot geometry the finished house would use. Then I narrowed the slot across five versions and assembled each one by hand.

At 2.90 the pieces went together easily and moved under handling. Working down through 2.75 and 2.70, the fifth version at 2.68 pressed home firmly, needed no excessive force, and held square while being handled.

What it changed That number went into every slot in the final structure. Across the whole range the difference is a fifth of a millimetre, and it decides whether the joint is loose, right, or too tight to assemble.
  • 2.90Assembles easily, shifts in the hand
  • 2.75Closer, still some play
  • 2.70Nearly there
  • 2.68Firm by hand, holds square

04

The model, and the sheet it becomes

The house exists twice: once as a solid model with the PCB cavity, the track and the tested slot width in it, and once as a flat sheet of cut paths. Getting from the first to the second is most of the work, because every layer in the stack has to appear as its own profile with its own slots.

How the stack works

Depth built from repeats

The wall is made by repeating two profiles: 25 window layers that carry the opening and the track, and 10 solid layers behind them. Changing how deep the track runs means changing a count, which is a far safer edit than redrawing a solid.

The rest is small, specific parts: roof connectors, floor connectors, slide side connectors, the back board, the board that carries the PCB, and the one with the cable hole in it.

05

Assembly and check

With no glue in the design, assembly is also the test. Each joint either holds at the slot width it was cut to or it does not, so the parts went together with calipers on the bench.

The mechanism

Captured from both sides

This is the fix from the cardboard test, cut: the window runs in a guided track and is held between an inner and an outer layer, so it can travel the length of the opening and cannot leave it. The part that presses the switch rides at the bottom of that travel.

06

Finished

Stacked layers, a window that slides, a board inside, and a cable out of the back. The object does one thing, and the whole build exists so that one thing feels deliberate.

Inside

Where the board sits

The PCB sits on its own board inside the cavity, positioned so the window's travel lands on the right mouse switch. The cable leaves through the back, which is why that one layer has a hole in it that none of the others do.

07

What I took from it

The idea was one sentence long: close a little window, enter focus mode. Making that sentence physically true took stability, motion constraints, dimensional accuracy and assembly tolerance, and that gap is the thing I actually learned.

How I work now

  1. I give each prototype one question. Cardboard answered the mechanism, a cut test answered the board fit, and five slot widths answered the joint.
  2. I test the real geometry, not a stand-in. The press-fit sample was a miniature of the actual beam, which is why its answer transferred.
  3. I treat the process as part of the dimension. The laser kerf was the reason the board fitted, and a drawing alone would not have told me that.

What I would try next

  1. Design the feel of the slide: how much resistance the window gives and what tells you it has reached the closed position.
  2. Make leaving focus mode as physical as entering it, using the same window rather than a second control.
  3. Carry the engraving test into the finish, now that I know what detail the material holds.
