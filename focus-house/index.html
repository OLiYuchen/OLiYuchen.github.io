Focus House

ConceptSketchesPrototypesFinal designReflection

01. Concept and Design Intent

A Little Ritual for Deep Work

Focus House is a small house shaped desk decoration designed to make entering focus mode feel more intentional and enjoyable. Instead of navigating a digital menu, users can slide a tiny window shut to signal that it is time to concentrate.

Inside the house is a repurposed mouse PCB. Closing the window presses the original right mouse button, which is mapped to a computer shortcut intended to activate macOS Focus Mode and switch the desktop wallpaper. The interaction creates both a physical gesture and a visual cue for transitioning into a focused workspace.

The design is intended for people who enjoy cozy desk accessories, thoughtful interactions, and small rituals in their daily routines. Rather than creating another conventional productivity tool, I wanted to make something decorative and personal that also serves a practical purpose.

I chose the shape of a house because it communicates comfort, privacy, and the feeling of creating a space for yourself. Its sliding window reinforces that metaphor. Closing it represents temporarily shutting out distractions. The stacked sheet construction also provides space to enclose the PCB and integrate a sliding mechanism.

02. Concept Sketches and Early Exploration

Sketch 01. Idea Exploration

I started by exploring different ways to repurpose the mouse PCB into a physical interaction instead of simply putting it into another mouse shaped enclosure. I wanted to create an object that could sit naturally on a desk and become part of someone's everyday routine.

This exploration helped me think about how an everyday physical action could communicate a digital transition. I eventually chose the idea of a small house with a sliding window, where closing the window would represent entering a focused state.

Sketch 02. House Form and Layer Design

After choosing the house concept, I sketched the form from different angles and started translating it into individual sheet material layers. I considered how to enclose the PCB, support the structure, guide the sliding window, and position the moving part so it could press the mouse button.

These drawings revealed important questions about structural stability and how the window would be constrained. They helped me decide what to test first with physical prototypes.

03. Low Fidelity Prototype

01. Size, Shape, and Sliding Mechanism

I made a quick cardboard prototype to test the structure and window mechanism before spending time on precise CAD modeling and laser cutting. At this stage, I was more interested in the relationships between moving parts than in accurate dimensions or appearance.

The first test showed that the supporting beams needed a more symmetrical arrangement and support in multiple directions to balance the house. I also discovered that two bars inserted into the wall were not enough to constrain the sliding window. The window could move along the intended path, but it could also fall out of the track.

I revised the design to include supporting elements arranged more evenly and additional layers on both the inside and outside of the slider. These layers capture the window within its track while still allowing it to move.

What I learned: A sliding mechanism needs both a path that guides motion and physical constraints that prevent unintended motion.

02. PCB Dimension Test

I used the PCB dimensions from my earlier sketch directly to prepare a laser cutting test. I also tried engraving during the same fabrication session to understand how the process could support the final design.

Both tests were successful. The PCB fit into the cut shape without major changes to the dimensions. I noticed that the slight material removal and darkened edges from the laser cutting process produced enough clearance for the PCB to sit inside the opening.

What I learned: Accurate CAD measurements are important, but the final fit is also affected by the laser cutting process and the material it removes.

03. Press Fit Joint Test

I tested a miniature version of the supporting beam structure from my actual design. Instead of using a generic joint, I made small pieces with slots that reflected the geometry I planned to use in the final house.

I gradually reduced the slot width across five versions, starting at 2.90 mm, then testing intermediate sizes including 2.75 mm and 2.70 mm. The fifth version, at 2.68 mm, provided the best result. The pieces pressed together firmly without excessive force and did not shift during handling.

I incorporated this tested fit into the final supporting structure. The small differences between iterations showed me how much dimensional tolerance matters when building with sheet materials.

What I learned: Press fit joints need physical testing. A change of just a fraction of a millimeter can determine whether a connection feels loose, secure, or too difficult to assemble.

04. Final Medium Fidelity Prototype: Focus House

Design Overview

Focus House uses stacked laser cut layers to enclose the original mouse PCB. The house serves as both a decorative desk accessory and a physical interface for entering Focus Mode.

Stacked Construction and Press Fit Assembly

Individual sheet material layers form the house and provide space for the PCB and sliding mechanism. The supporting structure uses the 2.68 mm press fit joints refined through five tests, allowing the layers to connect securely without glue.

Sliding Window Mechanism

The window slides along a guided track between inner and outer layers. This prevents it from falling out, solving a problem identified in the cardboard prototype.

PCB Integration and Interaction

The PCB is positioned inside the house so that closing the window presses its right mouse button. The input can trigger a computer shortcut to activate macOS Focus Mode and change the desktop wallpaper.

Decorative Intent

I chose the house form to create a cozy, personal desk accessory. Closing the window symbolizes shutting out distractions and turns entering Focus Mode into a small, intentional ritual.

05. Iteration Summary and Reflection

This project taught me that even a simple interaction requires careful consideration of structural stability, movement, dimensions, and assembly.

The cardboard prototype revealed that the supporting beams needed better balance and that the sliding window required constraints on both sides. The PCB test showed that CAD measurements translated well into laser cutting, while also highlighting how material removal affects the final fit. Through five press fit tests, I refined the slot width from 2.90 mm to 2.68 mm, achieving a secure connection without glue.

My biggest takeaway was learning to use each prototype to answer a specific design question. Rather than focusing only on making the final product, I learned how small experiments could guide better design decisions. And I enjoyed the beauty and neatness brought by precision.

If I continued developing Focus House, I would refine the sliding resistance and tactile feedback, making the interaction more satisfying and reliable. I would also explore a more intuitive way to both enter and exit Focus Mode.
